Motivation, Topic & Framing

Questions 1–6
1
Your title promises an investigation of product traceability, yet your scope statement restricts traceability to factory-to-warehouse movement only. In what sense is anything in Chapter 4 actually a traceability analysis — and would a more honest title not simply say shipment-level performance and return risk?
Hint

Do not argue the title is fine. Concede the gap between promise and delivery, then reframe what traceability you actually delivered.

Model answer

I accept the title oversells traceability. What I operationally delivered is shipment-level traceability — the factory-to-warehouse routing in the flow matrix, Table 4.5 — which is one layer of a full chain. A more precise title would be 'shipment-level performance and return-risk analytics'. I would defend the work on what it does measure: routing structure, delay profiles, and where return risk concentrates. If revising, I would either retitle, or add genuine traceability work such as lot-genealogy tracking across nodes.

Why it lands

Examiners reward candidates who can see the gap between promise and delivery. Defending an overclaim is fatal; owning it and offering a precise retitle is recoverable.

2
Chapter 1 builds its entire rationale on blockchain, IoT, ethical sourcing, forced labour and counterfeit goods — none of which appears in your data or analysis. Why open with a transparency-and-ethics argument you never operationalise, and how do you reconcile that framing with what you delivered?
Hint

Separate the motivating context (why the problem matters) from the analytical contribution (what you actually tested). Concede the framing was too broad.

Model answer

Chapter 1 sets the macro context for why transparency matters in fast fashion, but I agree it promises a blockchain, IoT and ethics agenda the data cannot deliver. The honest position is that this study is a narrow, data-driven slice of that larger agenda: it shows what operational analytics alone can and cannot reveal. I should have tightened Chapter 1 to the specific question I tested — whether shipment-level signals predict delay and return risk — and cited blockchain and IoT only as future enabling infrastructure, not as something my analysis engages.

Why it lands

The mismatch between framing and delivery is the most visible structural weakness in the thesis. The recovery is to recast the grand framing as context and future work, not as a claim the study supports.

3
If the problem you identify is multi-tier opacity beyond first-tier suppliers, how does a dataset of five factories and twenty warehouses speak to that problem at all?
Hint

Concede the data is single-tier. Argue what single-tier analytics still demonstrates and how the method is tier-agnostic.

Model answer

It does not address multi-tier opacity, and I should not claim it does. Five factories and twenty warehouses are a single tier — manufacturer to distribution. What the study can show is a method that, applied at each tier of an instrumented chain, would surface where return and delay risk concentrate. The multi-tier problem is the motivation; my contribution is a node-level analytic that is tier-agnostic and could be replicated upstream given the data.

Why it lands

The examiner wants you to admit the data’s scope ceiling without abandoning the method’s transferability — that balance is the mark of a thoughtful candidate.

4
You repeatedly cite blockchain as the remedy for the transparency gap. Given that your study uses none of it, what does your work actually contribute to the blockchain-traceability conversation you spend Chapter 1 building?
Hint

Be blunt: it contributes nothing to blockchain directly. State the real, smaller, complementary contribution.

Model answer

Directly, nothing, and I will not pretend otherwise. My contribution sits alongside the blockchain literature, not within it: it demonstrates the analytic layer that would consume blockchain-traced data once it exists. Put plainly, blockchain answers 'can we trust the record?'; my work asks 'given a trustworthy record, what risk patterns can we extract?'. Those are complementary, and I should have framed mine as the downstream analytics that justify investing in the traceability infrastructure.

Why it lands

Honesty about a null contribution to one literature, paired with a precise statement of contribution to another, is far stronger than vague claims of overlap.

5

Hint

Pick one primary audience and defend it; acknowledge the others are secondary.

Model answer

The primary audience is data-oriented supply-chain analysts and the practitioners who commission them — people deciding where to point analytic effort. The findings most relevant to them are the negative ones: delay is a poor return predictor, so do not over-invest in delay dashboards expecting return reduction. The methodologists’ interest is secondary — the leakage discussion is a cautionary tale. I concede the dissertation addresses all three loosely; if narrowing, I would write the recommendations strictly for the practitioner-analyst.

Why it lands

‘For everyone’ signals a lack of focus. Committees want a named reader and a contribution shaped to them.

6

Aims, Research Questions & Scope

Questions 7–11
7
Four of your five hypotheses were rejected. Does that outcome answer your research questions, or does it tell us the questions were poorly matched to what this dataset could support?
Hint

Reframe rejection as a finding, not a failure — but acknowledge it questions the hypotheses’ fit.

Model answer

Rejection is itself an answer: the research questions asked whether these relationships hold, and the answer is mostly no, which is informative — it redirects attention from delay to factory and product effects. That said, I accept the pattern suggests the directional hypotheses were drawn from consumer-returns literature that does not transfer to batch-level operational data. Why it lands

The committee tests whether you treat null results as legitimate science or as embarrassment. The right stance is the former, with self-awareness.

8
Objective 5 is ‘to assess the performance of machine-learning models.’ Assessment is a method, not a finding. What substantive knowledge claim does Objective 5 produce beyond a table of metrics?
Hint

Convert the assessment into a knowledge claim about asymmetric learnability.

Model answer

The substantive claim behind Objective 5 is that predictability is task-dependent: with the same feature set and pipeline, high-return batches were learnable — ROC-AUC around 0.92, though leakage-inflated — while delay risk was not, best F1 of 0.28. The knowledge is the asymmetry and its likely cause: return risk is encoded in volume and product features, delay is near-random at one-day resolution. The metrics table is the evidence; the claim is that not all operational risks are equally tractable from the same data.

Why it lands

Examiners push back on objectives phrased as activities. You must surface the proposition the activity establishes.

9
Your scope explicitly excludes return reasons, defect types, supplier and customer data. On what basis, then, can you make any claim about quality risk — a phrase that sits in your title?
Hint

Define your operational proxy for quality risk (return rate / high-return batch) and concede it is a proxy.

Model answer

I operationalised quality risk narrowly as return behaviour — return rate and the high-return-batch label — on the logic that returns proxy dissatisfaction or defect. I accept that is a thin proxy: without return reasons or defect types I cannot separate quality from sizing, fit or buyer’s remorse. So quality risk in my title should read as return-based risk, and I flag in limitations that true quality diagnosis needs the defect-level data I lacked.

Why it lands

The honest move is to name the proxy and its blind spots rather than defend ‘quality’ as if returns equalled defects.

10
‘What is the role of exploratory data analysis…’ — how is that a research question rather than a description of a technique you had already decided to use?
Hint

Concede the phrasing; restate the underlying empirical question crisply.

Model answer

I accept the phrasing is weak — ‘the role of EDA’ describes a tool. The real question underneath is empirical: what structure exists in the shipment flows, seasonality and return distribution before any modelling? Reworded, it would be ‘How are shipments routed, and how do delay and return behaviour vary across factories, warehouses, products and time?’ That is answerable and is what Section 4.4 actually addresses.

Why it lands

Committees reward candidates who can rewrite their own weak question into a sharp one on the spot.

11
Where is the boundary between what you set out to explain and what you set out merely to describe, and did your objectives blur it?
Hint

Be explicit: most of the thesis describes; the inferential chapter attempts to explain; concede the blur and where causal language crept in.

Model answer

Honestly, the boundary blurred. The EDA and anomaly sections are descriptive — they characterise patterns. The statistical and ML chapters attempt association and prediction. The blur is that I sometimes narrate descriptive patterns in causal-sounding language. The cleaner framing: EDA and anomaly detection describe; correlation, ANOVA and regression test associations; ML predicts — and none of it establishes causation, which I should have stated more consistently.

Why it lands

The examiner is probing causal overreach. The safe answer draws the description / association / causation line crisply.

C

Literature Review & Hypothesis Derivation

Questions 12–19
12
You derive H1 — delays increase returns — from Masuch et al. and Cui et al., then reject it. Was the hypothesis ever genuinely testable in your data, given that your entire delay range is only −2 to +2 days?
Hint

Argue the compressed range is exactly why the effect could not appear — a data limitation, not a refutation of theory.

Model answer

This is the key caveat on H1. With delay spanning only minus-two to plus-two days, there is almost no variance for a delay effect to express itself — a customer is unlikely to return because a parcel was one day late. So the near-zero correlation may reflect range restriction, not the absence of a real-world delay-return link. I therefore do not claim the theory is wrong; I claim that in this dataset, at this resolution, delay carries no return signal. A dataset with delays of days to weeks would be a fairer test.

Why it lands

Distinguishing ‘theory is false’ from ‘my data cannot test it’ is exactly the nuance examiners look for.

13
Much of your cited evidence concerns consumer-facing returns driven by customer expectations. Your records have no customer-level data at all. Is that literature even applicable to batch-level operational rows?
Hint

Concede the level mismatch (customer-level theory vs batch-level data) and explain the aggregation assumption that bridges it.

Model answer

There is a level mismatch. The returns literature is about individual customer psychology; my rows are batches with an aggregate return rate. I implicitly assumed batch return rate aggregates many individual decisions, so customer-level mechanisms should surface in aggregate — but aggregation washes out individual expectation effects, which partly explains why H1 and H4 failed. I should have drawn more on operations-level returns literature rather than consumer-behaviour studies.

Why it lands

Recognising the micro-to-macro aggregation problem shows methodological maturity and explains your own null results.

14
You cite several future-dated sources — Salehi et al. (2026), Meskini et al. (2026). How were these accessed and reviewed at the time of writing, and how confident are you in their standing?
Hint

Explain online-first / early-access publishing; offer DOIs; do not bluff.

Model answer

Those are journal articles published online-first with a 2026 issue assignment but available before the print date — standard in rolling-publication journals — so they were accessible and peer-reviewed when cited. If the committee wishes, I can show the DOI and online-publication date for each. I accept that future-dated citations look odd, and I should have noted ‘available online 2025’ to avoid the impression of citing unpublished work.

Why it lands

A calm, concrete explanation of online-first publishing defuses what looks like an error; offering DOIs signals confidence.

15
A large share of your references are SSRN working papers, ResearchGate uploads and academia.edu PDFs rather than peer-reviewed journals. How does that affect the evidentiary weight of your literature base?
Hint

Acknowledge the weighting issue; explain triangulation and which claims rest on peer-reviewed work.

Model answer

I accept a portion of my sources are working papers and repository uploads. My defence is that the load-bearing theoretical claims — RBV, Contingency, and the methods behind the statistical tests — rest on peer-reviewed journals, while the grey literature is used for contemporary context where peer review lags the fast-moving AI-in-supply-chain area. In a revision I would downgrade the grey sources to supporting citations and ensure every hypothesis is anchored to peer-reviewed evidence.

Why it lands

The examiner tests source discrimination. Showing you know which sources carry weight is the goal.

16
The review treats delay, factory heterogeneity, batch size and ML as four separate strands and claims integrating them is your gap. Did you actually integrate them analytically, or test each in isolation exactly as the prior literature did?
Hint

Concede you tested them largely in isolation; point to where integration did occur — the joint ML feature set.

Model answer

Fair challenge. The statistical chapter tests each strand separately, so there I replicate the siloed approach I criticised. The genuine integration is in the ML models, where delay, factory, warehouse, volume, product and seasonal features compete in a single model and the importance tables show their relative contribution. So the integration claim holds for the predictive layer but not the inferential one, and I should have been clearer that integration meant joint modelling, not four more separate tests.

Why it lands

Owning the partial nature of the integration, then pointing to where it is real, beats claiming full integration.

17
H3 predicts larger batches raise returns, on a scaling-inefficiency argument. You found the inverse. Did your review present any counter-evidence for an inverse relationship — and if not, was the review balanced?
Hint

Concede the review was one-sided on H3; offer the scale-efficiency / learning-curve explanation for the inverse finding.

Model answer

The review was one-sided — it presented only the scaling-inefficiency case, so when batch size correlated negatively with returns I had no theoretical anchor. The finding is plausibly scale efficiency or experience effects: larger, more standard batches may be better-controlled production lines. A balanced review would have cited the economies-of-scale and learning-curve literature alongside the inefficiency literature, which would have let me hypothesise either direction.

Why it lands

Admitting a confirmation-biased review and supplying the missing counter-theory shows you understand balanced scholarship.

18
You assert ‘a paucity of studies on micro-level batch data and machine learning’ in fast fashion. How exhaustive was your search, and can you name the two or three closest studies you are positioning against?
Hint

Describe your search strategy concretely; name adjacent works; narrow the gap claim.

Model answer

My search combined Scopus and Google Scholar on terms like fast-fashion returns prediction, supply-chain delay machine learning, and batch-level return risk, from 2020 onward. The closest works are Varma and Huang on ML and reverse logistics in fashion returns, and the apparel demand-forecasting work by Guan et al. and Limon et al. My gap claim is narrower than ‘nobody has done this’ — it is that micro-batch return-risk labelling combined with anomaly detection on a single operational dataset is under-explored. I would soften ‘paucity’ to that specific framing.

Why it lands

A vague gap claim is a classic vulnerability. Naming the nearest neighbours and narrowing the gap is the fix.

19
Your hypotheses are stated directionally — ‘positively associated.’ On what theoretical basis did you commit to a direction rather than testing two-tailed, given how weak the eventual effects turned out to be?
Hint

Tie direction to the literature’s directional claims; concede two-tailed would have been more conservative.

Model answer

The directions came straight from the cited literature, which makes directional claims — delays raise returns, scale raises defects — so one-tailed framing followed the theory. But given how contested those directions turned out to be in my data, two-tailed tests would have been the more conservative, honest choice, and they would not have changed any conclusion because the effects were trivially small regardless of direction.

Why it lands

Justifying the directional choice by theory, then conceding the conservative alternative, demonstrates statistical good faith.

Theoretical Framework — RBV & Contingency

Questions 20–25
20
Explain concretely how a correlation between batch size and return rate constitutes a test of the Resource-Based View. RBV concerns valuable, rare, inimitable resources — which of your variables is a resource in that sense?
Hint

Concede it is not a direct RBV test; reframe RBV as a lens for the predictive-capability finding, not the correlations.

Model answer

A batch-size correlation does not test RBV, and I accept that. RBV in my work applies only to the predictive-analytics capability itself: the idea that an analytics function able to flag high-return batches is a potentially valuable, hard-to-imitate organisational resource. The correlations and ANOVA are operational findings, not RBV tests. I over-stretched the theory to cover the whole chapter; it legitimately covers only the ‘is predictive capability a resource?’ question, and even there it is interpretive, not tested.

Why it lands

The examiner wants you to scope the theory honestly to the one claim it can bear, rather than dress every regression in RBV language.

21
RBV is a firm-level strategy theory; your unit of analysis is the individual shipment. Is there not a level-of-analysis mismatch between your theory and your data?
Hint

Acknowledge that shipment-level data cannot speak to firm-level resources; explain the intended bridge and what data would resolve it.

Model answer

Yes — RBV is firm-level, my unit is the shipment. The bridge I intended is that aggregated shipment analytics constitute a firm-level capability, so the resource lives at the firm even though the data lives at the shipment. But I concede I never measured anything at firm level — I effectively have one firm’s data — so RBV functions as an interpretive frame rather than something my design tests. A cross-firm dataset would be needed to actually test the resource-heterogeneity claim.

Why it lands

Naming the level mismatch and what data would resolve it is exactly the reflexive move examiners reward.

22
You argue that delay being hard and returns being easy to predict ‘supports Contingency Theory.’ Is that a genuine contingency-fit argument, or simply a restatement that one model worked and the other did not?
Hint

Concede the risk of circularity; specify what would make it a genuine contingency claim.

Model answer

There is a real risk it is circular — one model worked, one did not, therefore fit. To be a genuine Contingency claim I would need to show that performance varies with a measured contingency, for example that return-risk models work better in high-volume seasons than low ones, or differ by factory regime. I did not test that interaction, so I should present Contingency as a candidate explanation for the asymmetry, not as a tested result.

Why it lands

Distinguishing a tested contingency from a post-hoc label is the heart of the question.

23
Could the whole of Chapter 4 have been written without invoking RBV or Contingency Theory? If so, what work are these theories actually doing?
Hint

Answer honestly — yes — and explain what the theories nonetheless add as lenses.

Model answer

Honestly, yes — the empirical chapter stands on its own statistically. What the theories add is interpretive framing: RBV gives a reason to care that one risk is predictable (it is a potential capability), and Contingency gives language for why predictability is uneven. They are lenses, not load-bearing structure. I would rather state that openly than pretend the regressions depend on them.

Why it lands

Pretending theory is essential when it is decorative invites a harder follow-up. Candour about its interpretive role is safer.

24
Contingency Theory predicts performance depends on alignment between strategy and environment. Which environmental contingency variable did you measure, and where did you test that interaction?
Hint

Concede you did not operationalise an environmental contingency; name the candidate (seasonality / demand intensity).

Model answer

I did not operationalise a contingency variable or test an interaction — that is a genuine omission. The natural candidate in my data is demand intensity: season or monthly volume as the environment, interacted with predictive performance. Testing whether model accuracy or return risk shifts across high- versus low-demand months would operationalise Contingency properly. As submitted, the theory is asserted, not tested, and I would add that interaction in a revision.

Why it lands

Admitting the missing operationalisation while specifying exactly how to fix it earns credit.

25
If predictive capability is a ‘strategic resource’ under RBV, your own findings show it failed for delay risk. Does that falsify the RBV claim, or is RBV simply unfalsifiable inside your design?
Hint

Argue it neither confirms nor falsifies; flag the falsifiability concern openly.

Model answer

It does not falsify RBV, but I take the deeper point that RBV is hard to falsify here. The honest reading is conditional: predictive capability is only a valuable resource when the underlying signal is learnable; for delay it was not, so no resource was realised. That is consistent with RBV rather than a test of it. I accept my design cannot falsify RBV — to do so I would need a firm with strong analytics capability that nonetheless underperforms, which my single dataset cannot provide.

Why it lands

Acknowledging a theory’s unfalsifiability in your design is a sophisticated, examiner-pleasing concession.

E

Research Philosophy & Design

Questions 26–32
26
You justify positivism on the grounds that the variables have ‘stable relationships that can be measured objectively.’ Four rejected hypotheses say otherwise. Does that undermine your philosophical premise?
Hint

Separate positivism (relationships are measurable) from the assumption that they are strong; defend the former.

Model answer

Positivism only commits me to relationships being objectively measurable, not to their being strong or in the predicted direction. Finding weak or null relationships is a perfectly positivist result; I measured them objectively and reported them. What is undermined is my prior expectation, not the philosophy. If anything, the clean rejection of four hypotheses is positivism working — the data overruled the theory.

Why it lands

The examiner conflates positivism with ‘my hypotheses were right’. Separating them defends the design cleanly.

27
You acknowledge pragmatism is the rising paradigm in supply-chain research, then dismiss it. Given that your findings were largely negative and exploratory, would a pragmatist or abductive stance not have fit your actual process better?
Hint

Concede pragmatism’s appeal for the exploratory parts; defend positivism for the hypothesis tests.

Model answer

I take the point. The hypothesis-testing spine is genuinely positivist and appropriate. But the EDA and anomaly work are exploratory and abductive in spirit, and a pragmatist framing would have described that mixed character more honestly. A fully pragmatist stance would also have legitimised bringing in qualitative data to explain the ‘why’ questions I could not answer. So pragmatism would not replace my design, but it would describe it more truthfully than pure positivism does.

Why it lands

Granting the critique’s partial validity while defending the testing core shows philosophical literacy.

28
You call the design deductive because hypotheses preceded analysis — yet much of Chapter 4 is inductive pattern-hunting in EDA and anomaly detection. Is the design genuinely deductive, or a deductive frame laid over inductive work?
Hint

Concede the dual character; argue the overall logic is confirmatory with an exploratory phase.

Model answer

It is both, and I should say so. The hypotheses were specified before analysis, so the confirmatory spine is deductive. But EDA and anomaly detection are inductive — I let patterns emerge. The accurate label is a deductive study with an inductive exploratory phase. Calling it purely deductive overstates the tidiness; the honest description is hybrid, with the deductive tests as the primary contribution.

Why it lands

Examiners distrust over-clean methodological self-labelling. The hybrid admission is more credible.

29
Defend the cross-sectional label. Your data spans roughly ten months of 2023 in daily records. Why is that not longitudinal, and what did treating it as a snapshot cost you?
Hint

Defend cross-sectional by the design choice (no entity tracked over time); concede what longitudinal would add.

Model answer

I treat it as cross-sectional because I analyse the records as one accumulated snapshot — I do not track the same factory or product as a time series or model temporal dependence. The dates support seasonal grouping, not longitudinal inference. What a longitudinal treatment would add is exactly my future work: whether high-risk factories stay high-risk over time, and whether delay pressure is causally seasonal. With one partial year, I cannot make those claims, so cross-sectional is the honest label.

Why it lands

The distinction between ‘has dates’ and ‘is longitudinal’ is precisely what is being tested.

30
Your two most interesting unexplained findings are why ratings are independent of everything and why a handful of products dominate returns. Both are ‘why’ questions. Would a mixed-method design not have served your own research questions better than mono-method quantitative?
Hint

Concede openly; explain the data constraint that forced mono-method.

Model answer

I agree — my two most interesting findings are ‘why’ questions, and mono-method quantitative cannot answer them. The constraint was the data: a secondary, anonymised dataset with no access to the people or products behind it, so qualitative work was impossible after the fact. The right design, had I controlled data collection, would have been mixed methods — operational data for the patterns, interviews or product audits for the causes. I name this as a core limitation.

Why it lands

Conceding that the design could not answer your own best questions, and explaining why, is honest and earns sympathy.

31
Positivism assumes a detached researcher. Yet you engineered the variables, fixed the high-return threshold, and chose the anomaly contamination rate. Where is the researcher-independence your philosophy claims?
Hint

Reframe positivist objectivity as transparent, replicable procedure — not the absence of choices — and offer sensitivity checks.

Model answer

Objectivity in a positivist sense does not mean I made no choices; it means the choices are explicit, rule-based and replicable, so another analyst applying the same rules gets the same result. The threshold, the contamination rate and the feature definitions are all stated, so they are auditable. I accept these are researcher degrees of freedom, which is why I would add sensitivity analyses — varying the threshold and contamination — to show the conclusions do not hinge on my particular settings.

Why it lands

The mature view of objectivity is procedural transparency plus sensitivity checks, which is what the examiner wants to hear.

32
The Research Onion is a framing device, not a justification. Beyond citing its layers, what independent reasoning supports each methodological choice?
Hint

Agree; supply the first-principles justification for each layer briefly.

Model answer

Agreed — the Onion organises the choices but does not justify them. The independent justifications are: positivism because the variables are numeric and I am testing pre-set hypotheses; deduction because the hypotheses precede the data; mono-method quantitative because there is no qualitative data to mix; secondary analysis because the dataset already exists and suits the questions; cross-sectional because no entity is tracked over time. Each rests on the nature of the data and question, not on the diagram.

Why it lands

The examiner checks you can justify choices from first principles, not by appeal to a popular figure.

Dataset, Source & Validity

Questions 33–40
33
In Section 4.5.4 you describe this as a ‘synthetic supply chain,’ and in Chapter 5 you concede the Kaggle data may be simulated. If the data is synthetic, are your findings about fast fashion — or only about the rules of whoever generated the dataset?
Hint

Top priority. Concede openly; restate every empirical conclusion as conditional on data realism; pivot to the methodological contribution.

Model answer

This is the most important caveat in the thesis and I flag it myself. If the data is synthetic, my substantive findings describe the generator’s parameters, not the real fast-fashion world, so they cannot be read as evidence about real supply chains. What survives is the method: the feature engineering, the leakage diagnosis, the significance-versus-effect-size discipline, and the demonstration that some risks are learnable and others are not. I would reframe the whole contribution as a validated analytic pipeline awaiting real data, and downgrade every empirical claim to illustrative.

Why it lands

Pre-empting the fatal objection and converting the contribution from findings to method is the only durable defence.

34
Batch ratings correlate with nothing — delays, returns, volume — at R² = 0.00004. Is that not direct evidence the ratings were randomly generated? And if so, was H4 ever testable?
Hint

Concede the strong evidence; reclassify the null result as a dead-variable / data-quality finding.

Model answer

The evidence does point that way: an R-squared of 0.00004 against three predictors means the rating column behaves like noise. If ratings were randomly assigned, then H4 was never testable — you cannot detect a relationship with a variable that has no real signal. The honest conclusion is not ‘delays and returns do not affect ratings’ but ‘this rating variable carries no recoverable information,’ which is a data-quality finding, not a substantive one. I should have stated that distinction in the chapter.

Why it lands

Reclassifying a null result as a dead-variable finding is exactly the precision examiners reward.

35
February and March are entirely absent from your monthly summary. Why are two months missing from a dataset you describe as spanning 300 dates, and what does that do to every seasonal claim you make?
Hint

Concede the gap; state the impact on seasonal claims; admit you cannot explain the omission.

Model answer

Two months are missing and I cannot explain why from the source — it is a gap in the Kaggle file I should have flagged in the data description. Why it lands

Owning an unexplained data gap and bounding its effect is better than glossing over it.

36
Factory F004 ships to a single warehouse with 218 shipments against tens of millions of pieces elsewhere. That is not a plausible real factory. Does its presence not confirm the data is artificial — and why did you keep it inside your ANOVA?
Hint

Model answer

F004’s profile — 218 shipments to a single warehouse against tens of millions of pieces elsewhere — is not a plausible real factory, and it is one of several signs the data may be synthetic.
